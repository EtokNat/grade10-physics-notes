Surface Tension

Greenoak International School · Michaelmas 2026/2027

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Define surface tension as force per unit length across any line in the liquid surface, SI unit \(\text{N m}^{-1}\).
  2. Explain surface tension using the molecular model (surface molecule with net inward force).
  3. State three phenomena caused by surface tension.
  4. State how surface tension changes with temperature and with soap/detergent.
  5. Use the slider equation \(F = 2TL\) in a wire-frame apparatus.

Key definition — dual register

Note (WAEC form)

WAEC / WASSCE accepted form. Surface tension is the property of a liquid surface by virtue of which it behaves like a stretched elastic membrane. It is defined as the force per unit length acting at right angles to any imagined line drawn in the surface. SI unit: \(\text{N m}^{-1}\).

Mathematically: \(T = \dfrac{F}{L}\) where \(F\) is the force along a line of length \(L\) in the surface. [textbook form — mark-scheme wording not independently verified]

Notation. WAEC/WASSCE writes surface tension as \(T\); many textbooks (and Fig 3.3) use \(\gamma\). They mean the same quantity, same units (\(\text{N m}^{-1}\)).

IGCSE 0625 (2026–2028) note. Surface tension is not an explicit learning objective in the current 0625 specification. No 0625 question will ask for its definition directly — this is a WAEC-weighted topic. [Verify against 0625 syllabus 1.1, 1.3.]

Note (WAEC form)

Related term — viscosity (appears as a distractor in WAEC 1990 Q46 and the brush-bristle item below).

Viscosity is the internal friction between adjacent layers of a fluid in relative motion — the drag one layer feels as it slides past the next because of the cohesive forces between their molecules. It is the fluid’s resistance to flow.

  • High viscosity: honey, glycerine, engine oil — flow slowly.
  • Low viscosity: water, petrol, alcohol — flow freely.
  • Temperature dependence. For liquids, viscosity falls as temperature rises (molecules gain kinetic energy and slip past each other more easily). For gases, viscosity rises with temperature.
  • Not the same as surface tension. Surface tension acts along the free surface; viscosity acts within the body of a moving fluid. A still fluid has surface tension but shows no viscous force.

Full treatment belongs to the Fluids in Motion topic later this term.

Historical context

Honesty sidebar — not in IGCSE 0625 core

This topic prepares you for molecular descriptions of liquids used in IGCSE pressure/density questions, but no 0625 question will ask you for the definition of surface tension directly. All numerical and MCQ items below are WAEC/JAMB sourced.

Molecular model

  • A molecule inside a liquid is surrounded by other molecules in every direction. The cohesive forces balance to zero.
  • A molecule at the surface has liquid below but gas (air) above. The downward cohesive forces are unopposed → net inward force.
  • The surface therefore behaves like a stretched elastic skin: it tries to contract to the smallest possible area.

Phenomena caused by surface tension

  • Needle floats on water when laid flat (even though steel is denser than water) — the surface skin supports its weight.
  • Water strider walks on a pond by distributing its weight across hydrophobic feet.
  • Water droplets form spheres because a sphere has the smallest surface area for a given volume.
  • Water does not drip through an open silk umbrella (WAEC 1990) until it is touched — the surface tension holds a thin film across each pore.

The soap-film slider

A soap film has two surfaces (top and bottom). Each surface pulls the slider of length \(L\) with force \(TL\), so the total surface-tension force on the slider is

\[\boxed{\;F \;=\; 2\,T\,L\;}\]

Factors affecting surface tension

  • Temperature. \(T\) decreases as the liquid warms. Warm water has lower surface tension than cold water.
  • Soap / detergent. Dissolved surfactants decrease \(T\). That is why soapy water forms bubbles so easily.
  • Impurities. Most dissolved impurities lower \(T\); a notable exception is salt, which can slightly raise it.

Worked examples

Example 1 — I Do (phenomenon MCQ)

WAEC 1990 Q46. Water does not drip through an open umbrella of silk material unless the inside of the umbrella is touched. Which phenomenon is responsible? A Surface tension B Hydrostatic upthrust C Viscosity D Diffusion E Osmosis

Narration. The silk is woven with tiny pores. Water in each pore forms a surface skin whose tension supports the drop against gravity. A touch disturbs the skin and the drop falls through.

Answer: A.

Example 2 — I Do (numerical, \(F = 2TL\))

A rectangular wire frame holds a soap film. The slider is \(L = 5.0\ \text{cm}\) long. The surface tension of the soap solution is \(T = 0.025\ \text{N m}^{-1}\). Calculate the force on the slider due to the surface tension.

Narration. Convert \(L\) to metres: \(L = 5.0\ \text{cm} = 0.050\ \text{m}\). The film has two surfaces, so

\[F \;=\; 2\,T\,L \;=\; 2 \times 0.025\ \text{N m}^{-1} \times 0.050\ \text{m} \;=\; 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N}.\]

Answer: \(F = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N}\) (or \(2.5\ \text{mN}\)).

Example 3 — We Do (highest surface tension)

WAEC 1991 Q48. Which of the following has the highest surface tension? A cold water B soapy water C warm water D oily water E salty water

Cold-call reasoning. Surface tension decreases when water is warmed and when soap is added. Oil and other impurities also lower it. Salt raises it only slightly, but in the WAEC distractor set the intended answer is cold water.

Answer: A — cold water.

Common mistake

Common mistake — temperature direction

Students write “surface tension increases with temperature”. Correct: \(T\) decreases as a liquid warms (molecules move more vigorously and resist the inward pull less).

Common mistake

Common mistake — soap increases \(T\)

Students think detergent strengthens the surface because it forms bubbles. The opposite is true: detergent lowers \(T\). Soap bubbles form because \(T\) is low — a smaller force per unit length can be stretched into a bigger film.

Common mistake

Common mistake — forgetting the factor \(2\) in \(F = 2TL\)

A soap film has two surfaces (top face and bottom face). Each pulls with force \(TL\). Writing \(F = TL\) halves the correct answer. Figure 3.3 shows both surfaces explicitly.

Practice

  1. (WAEC 1997-style.) When a paint brush is removed from clean water, the bristles are pulled together. The responsible phenomenon is: A viscosity of air, B low density of water, C surface tension, D weight of bristles.
  2. (WAEC 1991 Q48, adapted.) Which of the following has the lowest surface tension? A cold water, B soapy water, C salty water, D rain water.
  3. (WAEC 1990 Q46 analogue.) Explain in one sentence why a steel needle, placed carefully on water, can float.
  4. A wire frame of slider length \(L = 8.0\ \text{cm}\) carries a soap film with \(T = 0.030\ \text{N m}^{-1}\). Calculate the force needed to hold the slider against the surface tension.

Answers: 1 — C; 2 — B (soapy); 3 — surface tension of the water supports the needle because the needle’s weight per unit length does not exceed the force per unit length of the surface skin; 4 — \(F = 2TL = 2 \times 0.030 \times 0.080 = \mathbf{4.8 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N}}\).

Key takeaways

  • \(T\) = force per unit length in the liquid surface, unit \(\text{N m}^{-1}\).
  • Molecular origin: surface molecules have unbalanced inward attraction.
  • Phenomena: needle floats, strider walks, droplet is spherical, umbrella holds water.
  • Temperature up → \(T\) down. Soap up → \(T\) down.
  • Slider on a soap film: \(F = 2TL\) (two surfaces).
