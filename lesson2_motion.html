Motion I — Lesson 2

Force & Circular Motion

Greenoak International School · Grade 10 Physics · Week 7

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Define force and state its SI unit.
  2. Describe the effects of a force and distinguish contact forces from force fields.
  3. Describe uniform circular motion and give everyday examples.
  4. State and use the formulas for centripetal acceleration (\(a = v^2/r\)) and centripetal force (\(F = mv^2/r\)).
  5. Distinguish centripetal from centrifugal force and relate them to Newton's third law.

Prior knowledge check

  • A body at rest stays at rest until acted on (Week 7, Lesson 1).
  • Motion can be translational (straight line) or rotational / circular (around an axis or in a loop).
  • SI unit of force is the newton (N). A 1 kg mass weighs about 10 N on the Earth.

Why do we need the idea of force?

  • A block on a table stays at rest until something pushes or pulls it.
  • A trolley gliding on a perfectly smooth, straight surface would keep moving uniformly unless something acted on it.
  • Something must cause a change of motion — starting, stopping, speeding up, slowing down, or turning.
  • That cause is a force.

What is a force?

A force is an agent that changes or tends to change the state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line of a body.

  • SI unit: the newton (N).
  • Common everyday words for a force: a push, a pull, a tension, a reaction.

Effects of a force

A force applied to a body can:

  1. Move a body at rest, or
  2. Stop or slow down a moving body, or
  3. Change the direction of a moving body, or
  4. Distort the shape of a body (bend, dent, stretch), or
  5. Change the size of the body (lengthen a spring, compress a sponge).

Two types of force

  1. Contact forces — the agent must touch the body to produce the effect.
  2. Force fields (non-contact forces) — the agent can act at a distance, with no direct touch.

Force fields can even act through a vacuum (the Sun's gravity reaches the Earth through empty space).

Examples side by side

Contact forcesForce fields (non-contact)
Push on a doorGravitational force
Pull on a ropeElectrical (electrostatic) force
Tension in a bow stringMagnetic force
Reaction from a table
Friction between two surfaces

Reaction: when you press on the table, the table pushes back on your hand with an equal force. That is a contact force.

Common mistake — weight vs reaction

A book sits on a table. Two forces act on it.

  • Its weight — the Earth pulling it down. This is a gravitational field force (no contact needed).
  • The reaction — the table pushing it up. This is a contact force.

Students often label the weight as a contact force. It is not: the Earth does not touch the book.

Worked example — Teacher's turn

Which of the following is NOT a consequence of a force field?

A Weight   B Surface tension   C Gravitational pull
D Magnetic force   E Electrical force

NSP Bk 1 p. 29 Q 11 (SSCE).

Reasoning. Weight = gravitational field. Gravitational pull = field. Magnetic = field. Electrical = field. Surface tension acts between touching molecules at a liquid surface — it is a contact effect.

Answer: B — Surface tension.

Circular Motion

Uniform circular motion

  • A body moves in a circle at constant speed.
  • Its direction changes continuously, even though its speed does not.

Examples:

  • A stone whirled on a string in a horizontal circle.
  • A satellite circling the Earth.
  • The Earth moving around the Sun; the Moon around the Earth.
  • A car driving round a circular roundabout at a steady speed.

Why there must be an acceleration

  • Velocity has both size and direction. Even with the speed unchanged, a change of direction is a change of velocity.
  • Any change of velocity means there is an acceleration.

Centripetal acceleration

Centripetal acceleration is the acceleration of a body in uniform circular motion. It is always directed toward the centre of the circle.

Its magnitude is

\[ a = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r} \]

  • \(v\) = constant speed along the circle (m s−1),
  • \(r\) = radius of the circle (m).
  • Unit of \(a\): m s−2.

Centripetal force

Centripetal force is the inward force required to keep a body moving at constant speed along a circular path. It is directed toward the centre of the circle.

From Newton's 2nd law (\(F = ma\)):

\[ F = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r} \]

  • \(m\) = mass of the body (kg); gives \(F\) in newtons (N).
  • Something real has to supply this inward force: the tension in a string, friction between tyre and road, gravity on a satellite.

What happens when the inward force is removed?

  • A stone whirled on a string flies off along the tangent the instant the string is cut.
  • Mud on a spinning car tyre flies off the tyre along the tangent once the speed is high enough that the stickiness can no longer hold it.

Applications — car on a curve, and banking

  • A car driving round a bend needs an inward (centripetal) force. Friction between the tyres and the road provides it.
  • On wet tarmac, friction is not enough — the car skids outwards along a tangent.
  • Banking the road (tilting it inwards) means part of the normal reaction points toward the centre of the bend. The road supplies the centripetal force even when friction is low.

Centrifugal force

  • By Newton's third law, every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
  • The string pulls the stone inward (centripetal). The stone pulls the string outward (centrifugal).
  • Centrifugal force is the equal and opposite reaction to the centripetal force.

Applications: a centrifuge uses centrifugal effect to separate heavier blood cells from lighter plasma; a spin-dryer uses it to throw water out of wet clothes.

Worked example — Teacher's turn

A mass of 10 kg is moving in a circular path of radius 2 m with a uniform speed of 50 m s−1. Find (a) the centripetal acceleration, (b) the centripetal force.

NSP Bk 1 p. 27 Example 2.5.

Solution.

(a) \( a = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r} = \dfrac{50^{2}}{2} = \dfrac{2500}{2} = \mathbf{1250~m~s^{-2}} \) (toward centre).

(b) \( F = ma = 10 \times 1250 = \mathbf{12\,500~N} \) (toward centre).

Key takeaways

  • A force starts, stops, redirects, distorts or re-sizes a body. SI unit: newton.
  • Two kinds: contact (push, pull, tension, reaction, friction) and force fields (gravitational, electrical, magnetic — act at a distance).
  • In uniform circular motion, speed is constant but direction (and so velocity) changes — there must be a centripetal acceleration \(a = v^{2}/r\) toward the centre.
  • The inward force producing this acceleration is the centripetal force \(F = mv^{2}/r\). Its Newton's-3rd-law reaction is the centrifugal force.
  • Friction supplies the centripetal force on a car bend; banking the road helps when friction is low.
