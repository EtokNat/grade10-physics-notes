Motion II — Lesson 1

Friction: Nature, Static & Kinetic, Laws of Solid Friction

Greenoak International School · Grade 10 Physics · Week 8

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Define friction and explain its origin at the surface between two bodies.
  2. Distinguish between static and kinetic (sliding / dynamic) friction.
  3. Define limiting frictional force.
  4. State the five laws of solid friction.
  5. Describe a simple demonstration that shows how frictional force builds up as the applied force is increased.

Prior knowledge check

  • A force is an agent that changes or tends to change the state of rest or uniform motion of a body (Lesson 2, last week).
  • When a body rests on a surface, the surface pushes back on the body with a normal reaction \(R\), equal to the body's weight on a level surface.
  • Contact forces act only where two surfaces touch.

Everyday observations

  • A book pushed gently across a table does not keep moving — it slows and stops.
  • A heavy box on the floor needs a big push before it even starts to slide.
  • Rubbing your palms vigorously makes them warm.
  • All three observations point to a force at the surface of contact that opposes relative motion. We call this force friction.

What is friction?

Friction is the force that acts at the surface of separation between two bodies in contact and opposes the relative motion of the two bodies.

  • It always acts along the surface of contact.
  • Its direction is opposite to the direction of motion (or attempted motion).
  • SI unit: the newton (N).

Why does friction exist? — the microscopic picture

No surface is truly smooth. Under a microscope, even a polished metal plate is a landscape of tiny bumps and hollows.

  • Bumps on one surface interlock with hollows on the other.
  • To slide one body over the other, you must shear through the interlocked bumps — that resistance is what you feel as friction.

A simple experiment to feel friction

Place a wooden block on a bench and attach a spring balance to it with a string. Pull horizontally, slowly increasing the force.

  • For small pulls the block does not move. The spring balance still reads a force — friction is matching the pull, keeping the block at rest.
  • As you pull harder, the balance reading rises — friction rises with it.

Static friction

Static friction is the friction that acts between two surfaces when there is no relative motion between them.

  • It is self-adjusting: it is exactly equal to the applied force, up to a certain maximum.
  • If the applied force is 2 N and the block does not move, the friction is 2 N — no more, no less.
  • If the applied force rises to 3 N and the block still does not move, the friction is now 3 N.

Limiting friction

  • As you keep increasing the pull, at some point the block is on the point of slipping.
  • The friction reading at that moment is the largest value that static friction can have for that pair of surfaces.

Limiting frictional force is the maximum frictional force between two surfaces when relative motion is just about to begin.

  • Any applied force greater than the limiting friction will set the body moving.

Kinetic (sliding / dynamic) friction

Kinetic (sliding or dynamic) friction is the friction that acts between two surfaces when there is relative motion between them.

  • Once the block is moving, the force needed to keep it moving at constant speed is noticeably smaller than the limiting (static) friction.
  • That is why a heavy box is hard to start sliding but easier to keep sliding.
  • In symbols: kinetic friction < limiting static friction.

How friction builds with applied force

Static friction matches the applied force up to a maximum (limiting), then drops to the smaller, roughly constant kinetic value once the body moves.

The five laws of solid friction

Law 1 — opposition

Friction opposes relative motion between the two surfaces in contact.

  • If you push a block to the right, friction on the block points to the left.
  • If the block is being pulled to the left, friction on the block points to the right.
  • Friction is never a driver of motion — it is always a resistance.

Law 2 — self-adjusting up to a limit

Static friction is equal in magnitude to the applied force, up to a maximum value called the limiting friction.

  • This is why you can lean on a wall, or rest a book on a slope, without either slipping — friction silently matches whatever force is pushing the body sideways.
  • Once the applied force exceeds the limiting value, the body begins to slide and kinetic friction takes over.

Law 3 — depends on the pair of surfaces

The frictional force depends on the nature of the two surfaces in contact — how rough, how clean, how polished, and what materials they are made of.

  • Rubber on tarmac has high friction (used in tyres, brake pads).
  • Ice on polished steel has very low friction (used for ice-skating).
  • The same block on glass, wood, or sandpaper will feel different friction values.

Law 4 — independent of area of contact

For two given surfaces, the frictional force is independent of the area of contact, provided the normal reaction is unchanged.

Lay the same brick flat (large area) or stand it on its end (small area): the force needed to pull it is the same. Only the weight presses down — not the area it is spread over.

Law 5 — proportional to the normal reaction

The limiting frictional force \(F\) is directly proportional to the normal reaction \(R\) between the surfaces:

\( \displaystyle F \propto R \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad F = \mu R \)

  • \(\mu\) (Greek letter mu) is a constant for a given pair of surfaces, called the coefficient of friction.
  • Double the weight on top of the block → double the normal reaction → double the limiting friction.
  • The detailed meaning of \(\mu\) and an experiment to measure it will be our focus next lesson.

Worked example (teacher-led)

Example. A wooden block rests on a horizontal bench. A horizontal pull of 2 N is applied to the block but the block does not move. The pull is increased slowly. When it reaches 5 N, the block is just about to slip.

(a) What is the frictional force on the block when the pull is 2 N?
(b) What is the limiting frictional force?
(c) Is the friction in (a) static or kinetic?

Solution.

(a) The block is at rest, so by Law 2 friction exactly matches the pull: \(F = \mathbf{2~\text{N}}\).

(b) Limiting friction is the value when the block is just about to slip: \(F_{\text{lim}} = \mathbf{5~\text{N}}\).

(c) Static — no relative motion yet.

  3. A block on a bench is pulled with a horizontal force of 3 N but remains at rest. The limiting friction between the block and the bench is 7 N. What is the frictional force on the block? Explain your answer in one sentence.

  • 1(a) Friction is the force at the surface of separation between two bodies in contact that opposes relative motion. (b) Limiting friction is the maximum static friction between two surfaces, reached when relative motion is just about to begin.

  • 3 — Friction = 3 N. Because the block is still at rest, static friction simply matches the applied force (3 N is well below the 7 N limit).

Key takeaways

  • Friction = force at the surface of separation of two bodies in contact that opposes relative motion.
  • Caused by interlocking of microscopic bumps on the two surfaces.
  • Static friction acts when there is no relative motion; it self-adjusts up to a maximum called the limiting friction.
  • Kinetic friction acts during sliding and is less than the limiting static friction.
  • Five laws: opposes motion; self-adjusts up to a limit; depends on surfaces; independent of area; \(F = \mu R\).
