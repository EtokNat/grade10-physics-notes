Part 1 · Friction in use

Is friction good or bad?

  • Without friction you could not walk — your foot would slip backwards every time it pushed off.
  • Without friction a car could not grip the road, a pencil could not write, a knot could not hold.
  • But friction also wastes energy in every engine, wears down moving parts, and heats them up.
  • So we increase friction where we need grip, and reduce it where we need smooth motion.

Advantages of friction (1) — walking & grip

  • When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backward.
  • By Newton's 3rd law the ground pushes your foot forward — this forward push is friction.
  • On icy or oily ground, that friction is too small, and you slip.

Advantages of friction (2) — vehicles

  • Tyres grip the road because of friction. Treads are cut into them to channel water away so friction stays high in rain.
  • Brakes work by deliberately creating friction: brake pads press against the wheel disc; friction converts the car's kinetic energy to heat and brings it to rest.
  • Climbing a hill or towing a load is impossible without enough grip between tyre and road.

Advantages of friction (3) — mechanical fastening & tools

  • Screws, nails and nuts stay in place because of friction between the threads and the surrounding material.
  • Fan belts (and conveyor belts) transmit power from a pulley to a shaft only because of the friction between belt and pulley.
  • A grindstone sharpens a blade by friction grinding metal off its edge.
  • A pencil writes because tiny particles of graphite are rubbed off onto the paper by friction.
  • Matches light by the friction heat of being struck.

Disadvantages of friction

  • Wasted energy. In every engine, part of the fuel's energy is turned into unwanted heat by friction at bearings, pistons and gears, lowering efficiency.
  • Wear. Moving parts rub each other and gradually lose material: tyre tread wears thin, brake pads wear away, shoe soles wear down.
  • Heat. Friction raises temperature. Overheated bearings can seize; overheated brakes can fade; machine parts can warp.
  • Noise. Friction between poorly fitted parts causes squeaks and grinding.

Part 2 · Reducing friction

Method 1 — lubrication

  • A thin layer of oil or grease fills the hollows between the two surfaces so their bumps no longer interlock.
  • The two surfaces now slide over the oil instead of over each other.
  • Used in engines, gearboxes, bicycle chains, door hinges.
  • Oil is preferred to water because water is too runny (too low in viscosity) to stay in the film.

Method 2 — ball & roller bearings

  • Instead of the shaft sliding inside its housing, it rolls on hard steel balls (or rollers).
  • Rolling contact gives far less friction than sliding contact.
  • Used in bicycle wheels, skateboard wheels, electric motors, car wheel hubs.

Method 3 — streamlining

  • When a body moves through air or water, the fluid has to flow around it. Resistance to that flow is fluid friction — a form of viscosity.
  • A streamlined shape (narrow in front, tapered at the back) lets the fluid slip past smoothly and cuts drag.
  • Used for cars, aircraft, trains, fish, submarines.

Method 4 — smoothing & polishing

  • Grinding and polishing a surface removes the taller microscopic bumps.
  • Fewer bumps to interlock → lower friction between the two surfaces.
  • Used for cylinder walls, bearing races, mirror mountings, ice-skate blades.
  • Note: smoothing alone cannot bring friction to zero — some bumps always remain, and extremely smooth surfaces can even cold-weld together. In practice, smoothing is combined with lubrication.

Part 3 · Viscosity recap

Viscosity — reminder from Week 6

Viscosity is the internal friction between layers of a liquid or gas in motion.

  • It is the fluid analogue of solid friction.
  • High-viscosity fluids (honey, glycerine, engine oil) flow slowly.
  • Low-viscosity fluids (water, petrol, alcohol) flow freely.
  • A body falling through a viscous fluid experiences a drag force. When the drag + upthrust balance the weight, the body falls at a steady terminal velocity:  \(V = W - U\)  (sphere at terminal velocity).

Viscosity vs temperature

  • Liquid viscosity falls as temperature rises: heat loosens the bonds between molecules, so layers slip past each other more easily. (Hot engine oil is thinner than cold engine oil.)
  • Gas viscosity rises as temperature rises: faster-moving molecules collide with one another more often, resisting flow more.

Solid friction vs viscosity

Feature Solid friction Viscosity
Where it actsBetween two solid surfacesBetween adjacent layers of a fluid
Depends on areaIndependent of areaDepends on area of contact
Depends on speedNearly independent of sliding speedIncreases with relative speed of layers
Effect of heatingLittle direct changeLiquid ↓, gas ↑
Common to bothOpposes relative motion; wastes mechanical energy as heat

Why oil is a lubricant but water isn't

  • A lubricant works by forming a stable film between the two sliding surfaces, so the surfaces never touch each other directly.
  • That film must stay in place under pressure — the fluid must be viscous enough not to be squeezed out.
  • Oil has a relatively high viscosity: it clings to metal and holds its film under load.
  • Water has very low viscosity: squeezed between two metal parts it is immediately pushed out, the metals touch again, and friction returns — plus water would corrode the metal.

Key takeaways

  • Friction is useful for walking, grip, braking, holding, writing, striking matches, driving fan belts.
  • Friction is harmful because it wastes energy as heat, wears parts, overheats bearings.
  • Reduce it by: lubrication, ball/roller bearings, streamlining, smoothing & polishing.
  • Viscosity is the fluid analogue of solid friction. Liquid viscosity falls with heating; gas viscosity rises.
  • Oil beats water as a lubricant because oil is viscous enough to hold a film under load.
