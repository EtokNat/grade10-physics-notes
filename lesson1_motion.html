Motion I — Lesson 1

Types of Motion & Relative Motion

Greenoak International School · Grade 10 Physics · Week 7

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Define motion in terms of change of position.
  2. List and describe the four main types of motion: random, translational, rotational, oscillatory.
  3. Give everyday examples of each type and recognise when two types combine in one motion.
  4. Define relative motion and identify the frame (point) of reference.
  5. Solve simple problems on the speed of a body relative to a second body.

Prior knowledge check

  • A body's position is located from a reference point using a coordinate system (Week 4).
  • Distance has magnitude only; displacement has magnitude and direction.
  • Units of distance: metre (m), kilometre (km); units of speed: m s−1, km h−1.

What is motion?

Motion is a change in the position of a body with time.

  • Kinematics — the description of how a body moves.
  • Dynamics — the study of why a body moves (the forces that cause motion).
  • In this chapter we start with kinematics, then move to dynamics (force, friction).

Four types of motion

  1. Random motion
  2. Translational motion
  3. Rotational motion
  4. Oscillatory (or vibratory) motion

A real body often shows two types at once, e.g. a spinning ping-pong ball flying through the air (translation + rotation).

1. Random motion

  • Objects move irregularly, at random, with no preferred direction.
  • Caused by many collisions between particles.

Examples: Brownian motion of smoke in air or pollen in water; gas particles in a closed container; a honey-bee buzzing around a flower bed.

2. Translational motion

  • A rigid body moves from one point in space to another without rotating.
  • Every part of the body follows the same path.

Examples: a diver falling straight down; a car from Enugu to Lagos; a train from Port Harcourt to Aba on a straight track.

Every point in the body stays fixed relative to every other point as the body moves.

3. Rotational motion

  • All points in the body move in concentric circles.
  • The centres of these circles lie on a line — the axis of rotation.

Examples: the Earth on its own axis; a spinning cricket ball or football; the blades of an electric fan; the wheels of a car (relative to the car body).

4. Oscillatory (vibratory) motion

  • A body moves to and fro about a fixed point, reversing direction regularly.
  • The motion is periodic — it repeats itself in equal time intervals.

Examples: the bob of a simple pendulum; a mass on a vibrating spring; the strings of a plucked guitar; the prongs of a struck tuning fork; a diving board after the diver jumps.

Combinations of motion

A single moving body can show two types of motion at once.

  • A ping-pong ball hit from one player to another — translation (through the air) + rotation (spinning about an axis).
  • A diver performing a somersault — translation (falling) + rotation (flipping about his waist).
  • A rolling wheel on a road — translation (whole wheel moves forward) + rotation (about its axle).

Common mistake — mixing up the four types

Students often confuse rotational with oscillatory motion because both involve a fixed point.

Use the mnemonic:

  • Rotational = goes all the way round the axis (fan blade, wheel).
  • Oscillatory = swings to and fro through a fixed point (pendulum, string).

Relative motion — why we need a reference

  • Motion is a change of position — but position measured from where?
  • We must choose a point (or frame) of reference assumed to be at rest.
  • Every measurement of motion is relative to that chosen reference.

Relative motion of a body A with respect to a body B is the motion of A as observed from B. B is the frame of reference.

Relative motion — the train & platform

  • You are sitting in a train that moves past a railway platform where your friend is standing.
  • Relative to the ground, you are moving.
  • Relative to the train seat, you are at rest.
  • Relative to your friend (standing on the platform), you and your friend are separating — there is relative motion between you.

Both statements are correct — they use different frames of reference.

Relative motion — man walking on a bus

A bus moves along a straight road at 100 km h−1. A man inside walks at 5 km h−1 relative to the bus.

  • If he walks toward the driver (same direction as the bus):
    speed relative to the ground = 100 + 5 = 105 km h−1.
  • If he walks back to his seat (opposite direction):
    speed relative to the ground = 100 − 5 = 95 km h−1.
  • Relative to the bus, his speed is 5 km h−1 in both cases — only the direction changes.

Relative motion — moving trees?

  • A pupil in a school bus looks at the floor of the bus. The bus appears to be stationary.
    Same speed as the pupil → no relative motion.
  • The pupil now looks out of the window. The trees appear to move backwards.
    Trees are at rest; the pupil is moving forward. Relative to the pupil, the trees move the other way.
  • The illusion of moving trees is caused by the relative motion between the pupil and the stationary trees.

Describing motion — using graphs

  • So far we have described motion in words: "the car moved from Enugu to Lagos", "the pupil saw trees moving backward".
  • To describe motion precisely, we draw graphs of how position (or velocity) changes with time.
  • Two graphs are used all through this topic:
  1. The distance–time (or displacement–time) graph.
  2. The velocity–time graph.

We introduce them here; we will use them in detail in Week 9.

Distance–time graph

  • Plot distance (or displacement) on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis.
  • A straight line means the body covers equal distances in equal times → uniform speed.
  • The gradient (slope) of the line gives the speed of the body.

Velocity–time graph

  • Plot velocity on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis.
  • A horizontal line → constant (uniform) velocity.
  • A line sloping upward → velocity increasing → acceleration.
  • A line sloping downward → velocity decreasing → retardation (deceleration).

Reading motion from graphs

  • On a distance–time graph: a steeper line means a faster body. A horizontal line means the body is at rest.
  • On a velocity–time graph: a steeper upward slope means greater acceleration. The slope itself is the acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity–time graph gives the total distance (or displacement) covered.

These three facts (gradient of d–t = speed; gradient of v–t = acceleration; area under v–t = distance) are the main tools for every motion calculation in Weeks 9–10.

Worked Example — Teacher's turn

A bus travels along a straight road at 100 km h−1. A conductor walks on the floor of the bus at 6 km h−1 in the same direction as the bus. Find his speed (a) relative to the road, (b) relative to the bus. (c) If he now walks at the same rate opposite to the bus, find his new speed relative to the road.

Based on NSP Bk 1 p. 28 Q 2.

Solution.

  • (a) Relative to road = 100 + 6 = 106 km h−1.
  • (b) Relative to bus = 6 km h−1 (same as the walking rate).
  • (c) Opposite direction: relative to road = 100 − 6 = 94 km h−1.

Worked Example 2 — Teacher's turn

Two buses leave a bus station at the same instant and travel in opposite directions from the same starting point. The speed of one is twice the speed of the other, and they are 240 km apart at the end of one hour. Find the speed of each bus.

NSP Bk 1 p. 29 Q 24.

Solution. Let the slower bus's speed = \(x\) km h−1. The faster bus's speed = \(2x\) km h−1.

In 1 hour, their separation = \(x + 2x = 3x\) km (because they move in opposite directions).

So \(3x = 240\) ⇒ \(x = 80\) km h−1.

Slower bus: 80 km h−1. Faster bus: 160 km h−1.

Key takeaways

  • Motion = change of position with time; described by kinematics, caused and explained by dynamics.
  • Four types: random, translational, rotational, oscillatory — often combined in one body.
  • Every statement of motion is relative to a chosen frame of reference.
  • Same direction → add the speeds. Opposite direction → subtract. Opposite-direction separation → add.
