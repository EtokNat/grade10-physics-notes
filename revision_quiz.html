Cluster 1

Fundamental & derived quantities · Dimensions

Week 1 · Q1 – Q16

Q1 · True / False

Mass is a fundamental physical quantity.

Answer. TRUE — mass is one of the seven SI fundamental quantities.

Q2 · Short answer

Name the SI unit of mass.

Answer. Kilogram (kg).

Q3 · Short answer

Name the SI unit of thermodynamic temperature.

Answer. Kelvin (K).

Q4 · Short answer

Name the SI unit of luminous intensity.

Answer. Candela (cd).

Q5 · Short answer

Classify acceleration as either a fundamental quantity or a derived quantity.

Answer. Derived — acceleration \(= \dfrac{\text{velocity}}{\text{time}}\), so it is built from the fundamental quantities length and time.

Q6 · Short answer

Give any two examples of fundamental physical quantities.

Answer. Any two of: length, mass, time, thermodynamic temperature, electric current, amount of substance, luminous intensity.

Q7 · Short answer

Which physical quantity is measured in newtons?

Answer. Force (also weight, which is a particular kind of force).

Q8 · Short answer

Write the dimensional formula for velocity.

Answer. \([\text{LT}^{-1}]\).

Q9 · Short answer

Write the dimensional formula for force.

Answer. \([\text{MLT}^{-2}]\).

Q10 · Short answer

Write the dimensional formula for pressure.

Answer. \([\text{ML}^{-1}\text{T}^{-2}]\) — because pressure = force/area = \([\text{MLT}^{-2}]/[\text{L}^{2}]\).

Q11 · True / False

Work and energy have the same dimensions.

Answer. TRUE — both are \([\text{ML}^{2}\text{T}^{-2}]\).

Q12 · True / False

Strain has dimensions of length.

Answer. FALSE — strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it is dimensionless.

Q13 · Short answer

Give one example of a dimensionless physical constant.

Answer. \(\pi\) (pi), or \(e\), or the refractive index, or any ratio of two like quantities.

Q14 · Derivation

Show that the SI derived unit of pressure can be written as \(\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}\).

Working.

\(\text{Pressure} = \dfrac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}\)

Force has SI unit \(\text{kg m s}^{-2}\); area has SI unit \(\text{m}^{2}\).

So \(\text{Pressure} = \dfrac{\text{kg m s}^{-2}}{\text{m}^{2}} = \text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}\). ✓

Q15 · Derivation

Derive the dimensional formula for power.

Working.

\(\text{Power} = \dfrac{\text{Work}}{\text{Time}} = \dfrac{[\text{ML}^{2}\text{T}^{-2}]}{[\text{T}]} = [\text{ML}^{2}\text{T}^{-3}]\).

Q16 · Essay

Define a fundamental quantity and a derived quantity. Give three examples of each.

Fundamental quantity. A physical quantity that cannot be expressed in terms of any other physical quantity. Examples: length, mass, time.

Derived quantity. A physical quantity obtained by combining two or more fundamental quantities. Examples: force, pressure, energy (also velocity, acceleration, density, power).

Cluster 2

Measuring instruments

Week 2 · Q17 – Q25

Q17 · Short answer

Name the instrument most suitable for measuring the length of a classroom.

Answer. A metre rule, or a measuring tape — long length, millimetre precision not required.

Q18 · Short answer

State one quantity that a vernier calliper can measure accurately.

Answer. The diameter of a small sphere (e.g. a marble), the internal diameter of a pipe, or the external diameter of a rod. Precision \(\approx 0.01\text{ cm}\).

Q19 · Short answer

Name the instrument most suitable for measuring the diameter of a thin wire.

Answer. A micrometer screw gauge — precision \(\approx 0.01\text{ mm}\).

Q20 · Short answer

State the smallest division (least count) of an ordinary metre rule.

Answer. 1 mm, which is 0.1 cm.

Q21 · Calculation

A vernier calliper shows a main-scale reading of \(2.4\text{ cm}\) and a vernier-scale reading of \(0.06\text{ cm}\). State the full reading.

Working. Full reading = main + vernier = \(2.4 + 0.06 = 2.46\text{ cm}\).

Q22 · Calculation

A vernier calliper shows a main-scale reading of \(3.1\text{ cm}\) and a vernier-scale reading of \(0.04\text{ cm}\). State the full reading.

Working. Full reading = \(3.1 + 0.04 = 3.14\text{ cm}\).

Q23 · Short answer

Name one object whose thickness is best measured with a micrometer screw gauge.

Answer. A thin sheet of paper, a razor blade, a sheet of metal foil, or a fine wire — anything in the range \(0.01\text{–}25\text{ mm}\).

Q24 · Calculation

Convert a time interval of 2 minutes 30 seconds into seconds.

Working. \(2 \times 60 + 30 = 120 + 30 = 150\text{ s}\).

Q25 · Calculation

Convert a time interval of 3 minutes 15 seconds into seconds.

Working. \(3 \times 60 + 15 = 180 + 15 = 195\text{ s}\).

Cluster 3

Molecular theory · Mass vs weight · States of matter

Weeks 1–2 · Q26 – Q30

Q26 · Short answer

State the molecular theory of matter in one sentence.

Answer. All matter is made up of very small discrete particles called molecules, which are in a state of constant random motion.

Q27 · Short answer

List three pieces of evidence for the particulate nature of matter.

Answer. Any three of: (i) diffusion of gases and liquids; (ii) Brownian motion of smoke/pollen particles; (iii) evaporation of a liquid; (iv) a solute dissolving in a solvent; (v) expansion on heating.

Q28 · True / False

A body's mass changes with location but its weight does not.

Answer. FALSE — it is the other way round. Mass is the same everywhere; weight changes with location because \(g\) changes.

Q29 · Short answer

State two differences between mass and weight.

Answer. Any two of:

  • Mass is a scalar; weight is a vector.
  • Mass is measured in kilograms (kg); weight is measured in newtons (N).
  • Mass is the same at every location; weight changes with \(g\) (so it varies with location).
  • Mass is measured with a beam balance; weight is measured with a spring balance.

Q30 · Short answer

State two differences between the molecular arrangement in a liquid and the molecular arrangement in a solid.

Answer. Any two of:

  • Molecules in a solid are tightly packed in a fixed lattice; molecules in a liquid are loosely packed and can slide past each other.
  • Solids have a definite shape; liquids take the shape of the container that holds them.
  • Intermolecular forces are stronger in solids than in liquids.
  • Molecules in a solid only vibrate about fixed positions; molecules in a liquid can move about throughout the liquid.

Cluster 4

Position · Distance · Displacement

Week 4 · Q31 – Q41

Q31 · Short answer

In one sentence, define the position of an object.

Answer. The location of the object relative to a fixed reference point.

Q32 · Short answer

In one sentence, define displacement.

Answer. The change in position of an object in a specified direction.

Q33 · Calculation

A student walks 5 m east and then 3 m west. Find (a) the total distance travelled; (b) the displacement.

Working.

(a) Distance is the total path length: \(5 + 3 = 8\text{ m}\).

(b) Displacement is the straight-line change in position: \(5 - 3 = 2\text{ m east}\).

Q34 · Short answer

An athlete completes one full lap of a circular track and returns to the starting point. State her displacement.

Answer. Zero — her initial and final positions are the same, so the change in position is zero.

Q35 · Calculation

A car moves 12 km north and then 5 km south. State its displacement.

Working. Net northward motion \(= 12 - 5 = 7\text{ km north}\).

Q36 · Calculation

A car moves 10 km east and then 4 km west. State its displacement.

Working. Net eastward motion \(= 10 - 4 = 6\text{ km east}\).

Q37 · True / False

The shortest straight-line length between the initial and final positions of an object is the magnitude of its displacement.

Answer. TRUE.

Q38 · Calculation

A person walks 6 m east and then 8 m north. Find the magnitude of the displacement.

Working. The two legs are perpendicular. Using Pythagoras:

\(|s| = \sqrt{6^{2} + 8^{2}} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\text{ m}\).

Q39 · Calculation

A boy walks 9 m east and then 12 m north. Find the magnitude of his displacement.

Working. \(|s| = \sqrt{9^{2} + 12^{2}} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15\text{ m}\).

Q40 · Short answer

State three differences between distance and displacement.

Answer. Any three of:

  • Distance is a scalar; displacement is a vector.
  • Distance is the total path length travelled; displacement is the straight-line change in position.
  • Distance is always positive; displacement may be positive, negative or zero.
  • Distance \(\ge\) magnitude of displacement (never less).
  • Distance does not depend on direction; displacement does.

Q41 · Calculation

A girl walks 12 km north, then 5 km east, then 7 km south. Find (i) the total distance travelled; (ii) the magnitude of her displacement; (iii) the direction of her displacement.

Working.

(i) Total distance \(= 12 + 5 + 7 = 24\text{ km}\).

(ii) Net north \(= 12 - 7 = 5\text{ km}\); net east \(= 5\text{ km}\). Magnitude \(= \sqrt{5^{2} + 5^{2}} = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07\text{ km}\).

(iii) Direction: \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{5}{5} = 1\), so \(\theta = 45^{\circ}\) east of north — i.e. N 45° E.

Cluster 5

Surface tension · Meniscus · Capillarity · Cohesion & adhesion

Week 5 · Q42 – Q54

Q42 · Short answer

Explain in one sentence why water rises in a clean, narrow glass tube.

Answer. Because the adhesion between the water and the glass is greater than the cohesion between the water molecules — so the water is pulled up the tube.

Q43 · Short answer

Explain in one sentence why mercury forms a convex meniscus in a glass tube.

Answer. Because the cohesion between the mercury molecules is greater than the adhesion between the mercury and the glass — the surface bulges upward.

Q44 · Short answer

What name is given to the curved upper surface a liquid forms in a container or tube?

Answer. The meniscus.

Q45 · True / False

A liquid that spreads easily over a solid surface is said to wet the surface.

Answer. TRUE.

Q46 · True / False

The surface tension of a liquid increases as its temperature rises.

Answer. FALSE — surface tension decreases as temperature rises (the intermolecular forces weaken).

Q47 · Short answer

Explain in one sentence why some small insects can rest on the surface of still water even though they are denser than water.

Answer. Because of surface tension — the surface of the water behaves like a thin elastic skin that can support the insect's weight.

Q48 · Short answer

Define surface tension.

Answer. Surface tension is the force per unit length acting along the surface of a liquid that makes the surface behave as if it were covered by a thin elastic skin.

Q49 · Short answer

State two everyday applications of surface tension.

Answer. Any two of:

  • The formation of spherical rain drops and liquid droplets.
  • Water striders (pond-skaters) and similar insects walking on water.
  • The cleansing action of soaps and detergents (they lower surface tension so water wets the dirt).
  • The wick of a kerosene lamp drawing up fuel.

Q50 · Short answer

State three ways in which the surface tension of a liquid can be reduced.

Answer.

  • Raise the temperature of the liquid.
  • Add soap or a detergent.
  • Add impurities such as grease, oil or dust.

Q51 · Short answer

Define capillarity.

Answer. Capillarity is the rise (or fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube — a capillary tube — dipped vertically into the liquid.

Q52 · Short answer

State three everyday applications of capillarity.

Answer. Any three of:

  • Ink being absorbed by blotting paper.
  • Oil rising up the wick of a lamp or a candle.
  • Sap and water rising from the roots of a plant up its stem.
  • A towel drying wet skin.
  • Melted wax rising up the wick of a candle.

Q53 · Short answer

Give one everyday example of cohesion and one of adhesion.

Answer.

  • Cohesion. Water droplets forming spherical beads on a waxed car surface; mercury forming a bead on a table.
  • Adhesion. Ink sticking to paper; chalk sticking to a chalkboard; water wetting the walls of a clean glass.

Q54 · Short answer

Differentiate between cohesion and adhesion.

Answer. Cohesion is the force of attraction between molecules of the same substance. Adhesion is the force of attraction between molecules of different substances.

Cluster 6

Viscosity · Upthrust · Archimedes · Flotation · Relative density

Week 6 · Q55 – Q65

Q55 · Short answer

Define viscosity.

Answer. Viscosity is the internal friction between layers of a fluid (liquid or gas) in relative motion.

Q56 · Short answer

State two practical applications of viscosity.

Answer. Any two of:

  • Lubrication of moving parts in engines and machinery (motor oil, grease).
  • Shock absorbers / dashpots — viscous damping of a vehicle's suspension.
  • Hydraulic brake fluid in a car's braking system.
  • Controlling the flow of paints, inks and syrups.

Q57 · Short answer

Define upthrust.

Answer. Upthrust is the upward force a fluid exerts on any body wholly or partially immersed in it. It arises because fluid pressure on the lower face of the body is greater than that on its upper face.

Q58 · Short answer

State Archimedes' principle.

Answer. When a body is wholly or partially immersed in a fluid, it experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces.

Q59 · Short answer

State the principle (law) of flotation.

Answer. A floating body displaces a weight of fluid equal to its own weight.

Q60 · Short answer

Define the relative density of a substance.

Answer. The relative density of a substance is the ratio of the density of that substance to the density of water (at the same temperature). It is a pure number and has no unit.

Q61 · Short answer

A metal ball of weight \(W\) falls through a column of glycerine. If the ball experiences an upthrust \(U\) and a viscous drag \(V\) at terminal velocity, write the relationship between \(W\), \(V\) and \(U\).

Answer. At terminal velocity the net force is zero, so weight is balanced by upthrust plus viscous drag:

\[\,W \;=\; U + V\,\]

Q62 · Calculation

A body weighs \(0.40\text{ N}\) in air, \(0.32\text{ N}\) when fully immersed in water and \(0.36\text{ N}\) when fully immersed in a certain liquid. Find (i) the loss of weight in water; (ii) the relative density of the body; (iii) the relative density of the liquid.

Working.

(i) Loss of weight in water \(= W_{a} - W_{w} = 0.40 - 0.32 = 0.08\text{ N}\).

(ii) \(\text{R.D. of body} = \dfrac{W_{a}}{W_{a} - W_{w}} = \dfrac{0.40}{0.08} = 5.0\).

(iii) Loss in liquid \(= 0.40 - 0.36 = 0.04\text{ N}\); \(\text{R.D. of liquid} = \dfrac{W_{a} - W_{L}}{W_{a} - W_{w}} = \dfrac{0.04}{0.08} = 0.5\).

Q63 · Calculation

A body weighs \(2.4\text{ N}\) in air, \(2.0\text{ N}\) when fully immersed in water and \(2.08\text{ N}\) when fully immersed in a liquid. Find (i) the loss of weight in water; (ii) the relative density of the body; (iii) the relative density of the liquid.

Working.

(i) Loss in water \(= 2.4 - 2.0 = 0.4\text{ N}\).

(ii) \(\text{R.D. of body} = \dfrac{2.4}{0.4} = 6.0\).

(iii) Loss in liquid \(= 2.4 - 2.08 = 0.32\text{ N}\); \(\text{R.D. of liquid} = \dfrac{0.32}{0.4} = 0.8\).

Q64 · Calculation

Ice has density \(\rho = 920\text{ kg m}^{-3}\) and floats in sea water of density \(\rho = 1030\text{ kg m}^{-3}\). Calculate the fraction of the ice that is submerged.

Working. Using the fraction-submerged formula:

\(\dfrac{V_{\text{sub}}}{V} = \dfrac{\rho_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_{\text{sw}}} = \dfrac{920}{1030} \approx 0.893\).

So about 89 % of the iceberg is under the water (11 % above).

Q65 · Short answer

How does the viscosity of (a) a liquid and (b) a gas change as its temperature rises? Give one reason for each.

Answer.

(a) Liquid. Viscosity decreases as temperature rises — the molecules gain kinetic energy and escape the intermolecular bonds more easily, so the layers slide past each other more freely.

(b) Gas. Viscosity increases as temperature rises — the molecules move faster, collisions between layers are more frequent, and this increases the internal friction.
