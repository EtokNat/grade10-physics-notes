Grade 10 Physics · Lesson 1 · Adhesion & Cohesion

Adhesion & Cohesion

Greenoak International School — Michaelmas 2026/2027

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Define adhesion and cohesion in terms of intermolecular attraction.
  2. Distinguish adhesion (unlike molecules) from cohesion (like molecules).
  3. Explain the concave meniscus of water and the convex meniscus of mercury.
  4. Give everyday examples of each force.

Prior knowledge check

  • SI unit of length is the metre (m).
  • Matter is particulate — solids, liquids and gases differ in packing.
  • A force is a push or a pull measured in newtons (N).

Key definitions — dual register

WAEC / WASSCE accepted form.

  • Fluid. Any substance — liquid or gas — that can flow and take the shape of its container.
  • Scope (“at rest”). This week we study fluids in equilibrium — not flowing. The branch is called hydrostatics.
  • Density \(\rho = m/V\); SI unit \(\text{kg m}^{-3}\). Water \(\approx 1000\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\); mercury \(\approx 13\,600\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\). \(\rho\) appears in every pressure and capillarity formula later this week.
  • Cohesion is the force of attraction between molecules of the same substance. [textbook form — mark-scheme wording not independently verified]
  • Adhesion is the force of attraction between molecules of different substances. [textbook form — mark-scheme wording not independently verified]
  • Meniscus. The curved upper surface of a liquid in a tube. Concave (dips in middle) when adhesion > cohesion (water in glass). Convex (bulges up) when cohesion > adhesion (mercury in glass).

IGCSE 0625 (2026–2028) note. Adhesion and cohesion are not an explicit learning objective in the current 0625 specification. The molecular model below is retained as background for the pressure and density topics that are examined. [Verify against 0625 syllabus section 1.1.]

Molecular model

The two molecules in a liquid feel each other through weak electrical attraction. When both molecules are the same kind (water–water, mercury–mercury), the force is cohesive. When the two particles are different (water–glass, water–paper, mercury–glass), the force is adhesive.

Rule of thumb.

  • If adhesion > cohesion, the liquid wets the surface and the meniscus is concave (water in glass).
  • If cohesion > adhesion, the liquid does not wet the surface and the meniscus is convex (mercury in glass).

Worked examples

Example 1 — I Do (teacher-led, concave meniscus)

WAEC 1988 Q40 — “Which of the following explains the concave meniscus of water in a glass tube?” A Adhesion water–glass > cohesion water–water. B Cohesion water–water > adhesion water–glass. C Molecules of water in the tube move faster. D Weight of water pulls the surface down.

Narration. The meniscus is concave — it curves upward where water meets glass. For the surface to pull up, the glass must be pulling the water molecules more strongly than the water pulls itself. That is adhesion > cohesion.

Answer: A.

Example 2 — I Do (teacher-led, definition MCQ)

WAEC 2002 — “The force between the molecules of a liquid in contact with that of a solid is…” A adhesive B cohesive C magnetic D repulsive

Narration. Two different substances in contact: liquid and solid. That is the textbook definition of adhesion.

Answer: A.

Example 3 — We Do (cold-call)

WAEC 1999 — “The force between molecules of the same substance is termed…” A elastic force B repulsive force C cohesive force D adhesive force

Cold-call three students. Expected answer: C — cohesive force.

Common mistake — inverted definitions

Students write “cohesion = different substances, adhesion = same”. Every WAEC MCQ places this inversion as a distractor. Use the mnemonic:

  • Cohesion → “Common substance” (same).
  • Adhesion → “Add a different substance” (unlike).

Common mistake — “cohesive force” muddled with elastic / repulsive

WAEC 1999 (Example 3) places elastic force, repulsive force, cohesive force, adhesive force as a single distractor set. Elastic and repulsive forces are not inter-molecular identity labels; they describe different physical situations. The only two labels that describe which pair of molecules is attracting are cohesive (same) and adhesive (different).

Everyday examples

Phenomenon Which force dominates? Why
Ink wetting paper Adhesion ink–paper > cohesion ink–ink ink spreads out
Water beading on waxed car Cohesion water–water > adhesion water–wax droplet forms a bead
Water climbing a clean glass test-tube wall Adhesion water–glass > cohesion water–water meniscus is concave
Mercury pooling as a droplet Cohesion mercury–mercury > adhesion mercury–glass meniscus is convex

Practice

Copy each question into your exercise book and answer there.

  1. (WAEC 2006 theory, part a — adapted.) Distinguish between the forces of adhesion and cohesion.
  2. (WAEC 1991 Q.) The boiling point of a liquid depends on all of the following except: A nature of the liquid, B external pressure, C volume of the liquid, D impurities, E molecular cohesive force.
  3. (WAEC 2004 Q.) When compared with water, mercury is preferred as a thermometric liquid because it: A has higher density, B has lower specific heat capacity, C exhibits anomalous expansion, D has strong adhesive forces.
  4. (JAMB 1978, adapted.) State one example of adhesion and one of cohesion from everyday life.

Answers: 1 — adhesion between unlike molecules, cohesion between like molecules; 2 — C; 3 — B; 4 — adhesion: ink on paper, chalk on board; cohesion: mercury bead, water bead on wax.

Key takeaways

  • \(\textbf{Cohesion}\): same substance; \(\textbf{Adhesion}\): different substances.
  • Meniscus shape = outcome of the adhesion-vs-cohesion contest at the liquid–solid interface.
  • Water + clean glass → adhesion wins → concave meniscus.
  • Mercury + glass → cohesion wins → convex meniscus.
