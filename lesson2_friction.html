Motion II — Lesson 2

Coefficient of Friction & Experimental Determination

Greenoak International School · Grade 10 Physics · Week 8

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. State the relation \(F = \mu R\) and define the coefficient of friction \(\mu\).
  2. Explain why \(\mu\) has no unit.
  3. Describe an experiment to determine the coefficient of static friction between two surfaces using a pulley and a scale pan.
  4. Interpret a graph of limiting friction \(F\) against normal reaction \(R\): the slope gives \(\mu\).
  5. Solve numerical problems involving \(F\), \(R\) and \(\mu\).

Prior knowledge check (last lesson)

  • Friction opposes relative motion.
  • Static friction self-adjusts up to a maximum — the limiting friction.
  • Kinetic friction (sliding) is less than limiting static friction.
  • Law 5: limiting friction is proportional to the normal reaction \(R\).

From proportionality to equation

Law 5 says: \(F \propto R\).

  • Any proportionality can be written as an equation with a constant:
\( \displaystyle F = \mu R \)
  • \(F\) = limiting frictional force (newtons)
  • \(R\) = normal reaction (newtons) — on a level surface this equals the weight pressing down
  • \(\mu\) = coefficient of friction between the two surfaces

What is the coefficient of friction?

The coefficient of friction between two surfaces is the ratio of the limiting frictional force to the normal reaction between the surfaces.

\( \displaystyle \mu = \dfrac{F}{R} \)

  • It is a ratio of two forces (N ÷ N), so it has no unit.
  • It is a property of the pair of surfaces — not of the block, not of the bench alone.
  • Rough surfaces → large \(\mu\) (close to 1). Smooth, lubricated surfaces → small \(\mu\) (much less than 1).

Typical values (for a feel)

Surfaces in contactApproximate \(\mu\)
Rubber on dry tarmac≈ 1.0
Wood on wood≈ 0.3 – 0.5
Steel on steel (dry)≈ 0.6
Steel on steel (oiled)≈ 0.05
Ice on ice≈ 0.03

Smaller \(\mu\) → easier to slide.

Measuring \(\mu\) in the laboratory

Experiment — apparatus

To measure the coefficient of static friction between a wooden block and a bench, we use a block on the bench connected via a string over a pulley to a scale pan.

  • Wooden block (known mass).
  • String over a frictionless pulley at the edge of the bench.
  • Scale pan (known mass) hanging from the other end.
  • A set of slotted weights to load the pan (and also the block).

Experiment — procedure

  1. Weigh the block; call its weight (plus any load on top) \(W\). Since the bench is level, the normal reaction is \(R = W\).
  2. Add small weights to the scale pan, one at a time.
  3. At some total pan weight, the block just starts to slide. That total pan weight is the limiting friction \(F\).
  4. Record the pair \((R, F)\).
  5. Add a known weight on top of the block. The normal reaction is now the new total weight of (block + load). Repeat steps 2–4.
  6. Record at least 5 or 6 pairs \((R, F)\) over a wide range of loads.

Experiment — the graph

Plot \(F\) (y-axis) against \(R\) (x-axis).

  • The graph is a straight line through the origin — this is the experimental proof of Law 5.
  • The slope of the line is \( \displaystyle \mu = \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta R}\).

Why the pulley + pan method works

  • The hanging pan pulls the string with a force equal to its own weight (and its load).
  • The pulley redirects that pull to act horizontally on the block.
  • So the horizontal pull on the block is simply (weight of pan + load on pan).
  • When the block just slides, that horizontal pull equals the limiting friction \(F\).

Sources of error to mention to students: pulley friction (never zero), slight jerk when adding weights, uneven bench surface, dust on contact area.

Worked example — NSP 2.3

A block of wood of weight 4 N rests on a horizontal table. When a horizontal force of 2.5 N is applied, the block just begins to slide.

(a) What is the coefficient of static friction between the block and the table?
(b) If a horizontal force of only 2 N were applied instead, what is the frictional force on the block?

Solution.

  • The surface is level, so normal reaction \( R = W = 4\text{ N}\).
  • Limiting friction \(F = 2.5\text{ N}\) (the pull that just starts sliding).
  • (a) \( \displaystyle \mu = \dfrac{F}{R} = \dfrac{2.5}{4} = \mathbf{0.625} \)  (no unit).
  • (b) 2 N is less than the 2.5 N limit. The block does not move, so friction simply matches the pull: \(\mathbf{F = 2\text{ N}}\).

Common slip to avoid

\( F = \mu R \) gives the limiting friction — the maximum value static friction can reach.

  • If the applied pull is less than \(\mu R\), the actual friction equals the pull, not \(\mu R\).
  • Only use \(F = \mu R\) when the question says "just about to slide", "on the point of slipping", "maximum friction", or the body is already sliding.
  • In NSP 2.3(b), the pull (2 N) is below the limit (2.5 N) — so the answer is 2 N, not 2.5 N.

Class task

Copy each question into your exercise book and answer there.

  1. Define the coefficient of friction and state why it has no unit.
  2. A block of mass 2 kg rests on a horizontal bench. The coefficient of static friction between the block and the bench is 0.4. Taking \(g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}\), find the minimum horizontal force needed to start the block moving.

Expected answers:

  • 1 — \(\mu\) is the ratio of limiting friction to the normal reaction (\(\mu = F/R\)). Because it is the ratio of two forces (both in newtons), the units cancel, leaving no unit.
  • 2 — Weight \(W = mg = 2 \times 10 = 20\text{ N}\). On a level bench, \(R = W = 20\text{ N}\). Minimum force to start sliding = limiting friction \( = \mu R = 0.4 \times 20 = \mathbf{8\text{ N}}\).

Key takeaways

  • Limiting friction: \( F = \mu R \).
  • Coefficient of friction: \( \mu = F/R \) — a ratio, so no unit.
  • Measured in the lab with a block + string + pulley + scale pan, varying the load.
  • Graph of \(F\) against \(R\) is a straight line through the origin; slope = \(\mu\).
  • Only use \(F = \mu R\) at (or beyond) the point of slipping — below that, friction equals the applied force.
